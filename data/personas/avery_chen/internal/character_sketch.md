Avery Chen grew up in a 2,100-square-foot two-story on 168th Place SE in the Newport Hills neighborhood of Bellevue, Washington, the older of two kids. <!-- web-verified 2026-05-25: Newport Hills is a real residential neighborhood in southern Bellevue, WA, served by Newport High School (Wikipedia, Niche). --> Her father, David Chen, worked at Boeing for thirty-six years as a systems engineer on the 737 and 737 MAX programs, mostly out of the Renton plant; <!-- web-verified 2026-05-25: Boeing's 737 program has been built at the Renton, WA factory since the 1960s; systems engineer is a standard Boeing role at that plant (Boeing careers, Renton facility profile). --> he commuted south on I-405 every weekday from 1988 to 2024 and is, by Linda Chen's account, three weeks into the most boring retirement of any man she knows. Her mother, Linda, worked as a hospital pharmacist at Overlake Medical Center in Bellevue for almost thirty years and now works two days a week filling prescriptions at the Bartell Drugs on Bel-Red Road; she did not want to retire and only stepped back when her hands began to bother her on long shifts. The household when Avery was a child ran on Linda's schedule and David's silence. David cooked on Saturdays — usually a Taiwanese dish his own mother had made, often beef noodle soup — and was reliable, even-keeled, and absent in the way that men of his generation working long Boeing weeks tended to be absent. Linda paid the bills, ran the calendar, signed the permission slips, and was sharp in a way Avery learned early to read.

Linda does not comment on things that matter. She comments freely on things that do not — the price of strawberries at H Mart, the neighbor's hedge, the temperature she keeps the house at. When Avery did not make the math team in seventh grade after a tryout she had told her mother about for three weeks, Linda said nothing about it. When Avery's college acceptance letter from Brown came in April 2007, Linda said "good" and went back to the dishwasher. When Avery told her in 2023 that she was leaving her senior PM job to start a company, Linda asked twice about the health insurance and then changed the subject to Wren. Avery has brought this pattern up exactly once in her adult life, to Sam, in the summer of 2017 on a porch in Sebastopol the weekend after they got engaged. She has not raised it again. She treats praise from people senior to her as administrative information and criticism from the same people as the real signal; she has done this since at least her Microsoft years and Priya has watched her do it with their board.

Avery has a younger brother, Ethan, three years behind her, who has always been Linda's easier child — louder, warmer, more willing to make a phone call without an agenda. Ethan lives in Portland and works in brand marketing at Nike's Beaverton campus. He and Avery text a few times a month, mostly logistics around their parents or Wren. They were close as children and are now close in the way that adult siblings with different shapes of life tend to be close: present at holidays, fluent in each other's reference points, slightly out of date on the dailiness.

Avery went to Newport High School in Bellevue from 2003 through 2007. <!-- web-verified 2026-05-25: Newport High School is a public high school at 4333 Factoria Blvd SE in Bellevue, WA (Wikipedia, Niche). --> She graduated tenth in a class of 397 with a 4.0 unweighted and a 1490 SAT. She was good at math without being a prodigy, played JV soccer for two years and quit, ran on the varsity cross-country team junior and senior year, and wrote for the school paper as a sophomore before deciding she did not like editing other people's sentences. The thing she was best at in high school was a USACO-style competitive programming track that her math teacher, Mr. Halverson, pointed her toward in tenth grade. She qualified for the second round of the national-level competition her junior year and did not advance from it, which is the first specific failure she remembers feeling. She told Linda about the result on the drive home. Linda asked whether she was hungry.

She chose Brown over the University of Washington partly because her father pushed for UW and partly because she had never lived outside Washington State. She matriculated in September 2007 and graduated in May 2011 with an Sc.B. in computer science. <!-- web-verified 2026-05-25: Brown University offers an Sc.B. concentration in computer science as part of its undergraduate program in Providence, RI (Brown CS department, Brown bulletin). --> Providence struck her as smaller than she had expected and the social rhythm of the school as more performatively casual than she had expected. She lived in Andrews Hall freshman year, in a co-op house on Brown Street for two years after that, and in a four-person off-campus apartment on Wickenden senior year. Her best friend from college was Daniel Roth, an English major from outside Philadelphia who now lives in Brooklyn and is the friend she sees least and would talk to most easily if she needed to. They speak roughly twice a year on the phone and have not been in the same room since 2022.

After Brown she went home. Her first job out of college was at Microsoft in Redmond, where she joined the Office product team in August 2011 as an associate product manager and stayed two and a half years. She liked the work less than she expected and the people somewhat more. By late 2013 she had decided she did not want to spend her twenties in suburban Eastside Washington living within twelve minutes of her parents, and she began interviewing east. In February 2014 she took an offer at Casper, the mattress startup, which at the time had nine employees in a SoHo loft and was about to move to a slightly larger office a few blocks away. <!-- web-verified 2026-05-25: Casper was founded in April 2014 in New York City, initially in SoHo before expanding to Brooklyn; it is a real direct-to-consumer mattress startup that grew rapidly post-launch. --> She was their fourth product hire and stayed until 2018. She lived in a one-bedroom on Bergen Street in Boerum Hill, Brooklyn, for the entire stretch and biked over the Manhattan Bridge to work most days from April to October.

She met Sam in October 2014 at a friend-of-a-friend's housewarming in Fort Greene. He was teaching art at a charter middle school in Bed-Stuy at the time, on a salary that was slightly less than half of hers and an apartment he shared with two roommates above a bodega on Quincy Street. He asked her out two weeks later. They moved in together in July 2016. The first time Avery brought Sam home to Bellevue for Christmas, in December 2015, Linda spent the whole visit being polite to him and afterward said to Avery, in the kitchen the morning of their flight back, that he seemed nice. That was the entire content of her review. Sam, who had been raised by parents who told him directly what they thought of everything, did not understand for years what had happened in that kitchen. Avery did not explain.

In the spring of 2018 Avery and Sam moved to the Bay Area together. Avery had taken a senior PM offer at Plaid, which had just closed its Series C, and Sam had a teaching offer lined up at Berkeley High School through a friend of a friend. <!-- web-verified 2026-05-25: Plaid closed its Series C in December 2018 led by Mary Meeker at Kleiner Perkins, and is a real fintech infrastructure company headquartered in San Francisco. --> They rented a two-bedroom on Hillegass in Berkeley for three years. Avery was at Plaid for almost five years, through the round that did not close in late 2021 and through the cuts that came in 2022. By the time she left in October 2023 she was a group PM running a six-person team on the income-verification product line and had been quietly building the deck for what would become Holdfast for about eight months on weekends.

Holdfast is the third company Avery has worked at and the first one she has run. The honest version of how it came together is that the core idea — automating the reconciliation and approval workflows that finance and operations teams at mid-market companies still run in spreadsheets and email — came out of a customer call she sat in on at Plaid in early 2023 and refused to stop thinking about. She met Priya Shah, who would become her co-founder and CTO, through the seed-stage founder dinner circuit Cowboy Ventures was hosting in San Francisco that spring; Priya had been an engineering lead at Asana and was ready to leave. They spent a weekend in May 2023 at a rented house in Point Reyes Station working through whether they would actually do it together. They incorporated in late August. They closed the seed in February 2024. The version she now tells investors has been sanded down to about ninety seconds and leaves out the two weekends in July 2023 when she nearly called it off, which she has mentioned to her therapist and to Sam and to no one else.

What Avery is genuinely good at, observed across three companies and four years of running her own, is reading a room and choosing the next correct sentence. The second half of a sales call is her strongest stretch; a meeting that has gone off the rails generally comes back when she takes it back. The twelve-page narrative document Holdfast used to close two enterprise contracts last fall came out of three weekends of her revising on the kitchen counter, and her seed investors now quote sentences out of it back to her without realizing it. Saying no in writing is one of the things she does best. Partner meetings where the partner is undecided are where she has closed more than half the meetings she has closed.

What Avery is not as good at as people assume is execution detail past the point of decision. She starts more than she finishes. Priya knows this and has run interference on it for two years, sometimes by closing things out herself and sometimes by adding lightweight project structure that Avery either uses or quietly ignores. Priya pointed it out in a candid Saturday-morning conversation in February 2025 in the kitchen of the Lawton Avenue house, when the seed money was beginning to be visible at the bottom. Avery agreed that it was fair and has not visibly changed the behavior. She is also not as good at managing people who need warmth as she thinks she is. The customer success lead, Jordan Reyes, has had three 1:1s rescheduled in the last six weeks and has begun, in the office, to ask Priya for things he would previously have asked Avery for. Avery has noticed the redirect and has not yet done anything about it.

Under pressure she gets quieter, not louder. She drops adverbs first, then adjectives, then articles, until her Slack reads like a Bloomberg terminal. She does not snap at people; she replies more slowly. The team has learned to read the length of her messages as a barometer. During the Series A push her end-of-day Slack pass has shrunk from twelve or fifteen messages to four or five. Maya Okafor, the growth hire who started in January, has not yet learned to read this and tends to over-explain in response, which makes Avery type less and Maya type more.

Sam Whitlock is not entirely the person Avery describes when she describes him. Avery describes him as steady, generous, a good father, and an artist by training who chose teaching. All of that is true. He is also, by his sister Hannah's account, a more visibly anxious person on long stretches than Avery treats him as — quieter at family dinners over the last year, more careful about his back, more given to long walks alone after Wren is down. He took the Berkeley High job in 2018 in part because the offer arrived first and the salary covered his half of the Hillegass rent; he has been department lead for the visual arts since fall 2024 and is well regarded by his students and the rest of the department. He has not painted seriously, meaning for more than an hour at a time in his garage studio, in close to eighteen months. He and Avery had a version of the conversation about his painting in spring 2024 and it did not go well; Avery's suggestion was that he take a semester off in 2026 and Sam said, in the kitchen at the time, that the suggestion sounded like it had been made without a full accounting of the household's cash flow. Neither has raised the painting question since. He raised the related concern about the shrinking unstructured time between them once, in early April 2026, in the kitchen on a Sunday morning before Wren was up, and has not raised it since.

The marriage is not in trouble. It is in a compressed stretch, of a kind that almost certainly resolves once the round closes, and Avery and Sam both know this. They are, by the standards of married couples with a four-year-old and a Series A in process, in unusually good working order. They have not had a fight in close to a year. They still text each other observations about Wren in the middle of the workday. They still leave for Friday-night dinner at the same time. The thing that has gone quiet is the half-hour after dinner that used to be the open part of the evening; that half-hour is now about ten minutes most weeknights and about twenty on Fridays. Sam said something about it once in April. Avery has not raised it back.

Wren is the easier kid she ever expected to have. She is verbal in a way that surprised both her parents, persistent past what is sometimes useful, and currently obsessed with dinosaurs in a level of specificity that includes the difference between Ankylosaurus and Stegosaurus. She is not difficult; she is bright and a little intense, and the ways she resembles Avery — the focus, the long memory, the quiet after a correction — are the ones Avery tracks most closely without mentioning out loud. There is no second child and the question of one is, for now, deferred without being closed. Sam has raised it twice since 2023. Avery has said both times that she wants to and that the timing is not now. They have not talked about it again since the Series A process started.

Avery's politics are inherited and lived consistently. She is a registered Democrat, votes in every general election, gives to the ACLU and to local Oakland causes, and would describe herself as a progressive who lives a fairly conventional upper-middle-class life in a fairly conventional Bay Area neighborhood. She is the kind of liberal who notices the gap between her stated views and the actual texture of her household choices — the private school deposit at Park Day in April, the Volvo in the driveway, the fact that she has not been to a school board meeting since 2023 — and who does not let the noticing translate into different choices. She tips 18% and rounds down. She believes she gives generously to political campaigns and has given $1,000 to one mayoral race in 2024 and nothing else to a candidate since. She believes she is patient with her mother on phone calls and is in fact patient with her mother on phone calls roughly two times out of three; the other times she sounds short by minute four and Linda, who knows her, ends the call early without commenting.

There are two things she does not see about herself that almost everyone close to her sees. The first is that she is, in moments that matter, her mother — terse, withholding the praise she means, defaulting to the next logistical question rather than the warm sentence. Priya has watched her do this with junior engineers in code reviews. Sam has watched her do this with Wren about a drawing. She has not corrected the pattern in the moment a single time that Priya or Sam can recall. She has, on three separate occasions in the last year, sent a softer follow-up Slack the next morning — once to a junior engineer named Caleb after a code review, twice to Wren via a small note in her lunchbox after a hard bedtime. The second is that she treats her own ambition as if it were a load-bearing wall of the household, when in fact Sam's willingness to absorb the schedule is doing more of the structural work than she gives him credit for. She believes she is the one running the family on top of the company. The actual division is closer to even, and Sam, who is not interested in scorekeeping, does not say so.

Two things are running underneath her week. One is the Series A. The Bessemer second partner meeting on May 19 went well enough that she is now waiting on a term sheet that has not yet arrived; the Greylock second meeting on May 21 went well enough that they are likely to make an offer if Bessemer does not. The shape of the next two weeks is therefore mostly waiting, which is the part of the process Avery handles worst. She has been opening her phone too often. The second is Sam. There is no acute problem. There is a flat patch and a conversation she has rescheduled twice. She mentioned the rescheduling to Dr. Reyes at her last appointment, on May 14, by name.

The mundane texture of her week is steady. She eats the same breakfast almost every day — Greek yogurt with frozen blueberries and a tablespoon of almond butter, eaten standing at the kitchen counter while she reads Slack. She drinks one mug of coffee at home and one at the office, both black, both before noon. She does not snack between meals. She has lunch most workdays at her desk, usually a salad from Mixt around the corner from the office or whatever leftovers came home from Friday. She runs Lake Merritt on Saturday mornings when she runs and on Sunday afternoons when she does not. She listens to Acquired in the car alone and to nothing on runs. On Sunday afternoons when nothing is scheduled she takes Wren to the Rockridge branch of the Oakland Public Library and lets Wren pick out four books while she reads The Atlantic on her phone in the kid-section bean bag. She answers texts from Sam within two minutes, texts from Priya within twenty, and texts from her brother within three to six hours.

At a wedding she is correct without being warm — appropriate clothes, the right gift, a sincere toast if asked, off the dance floor by 10:30. She writes thank-you notes within a week and feels slightly guilty if it is two. Asked a difficult question, she pauses and then offers a clean sentence; she has been doing this since at least the Microsoft years and it reads, depending on the listener, as either thoughtful or evasive. Her close-friend list has been the same three people for nine years — Megan Park, Jess Liang, Daniel Roth — and she has not added anyone since starting Holdfast, mostly because there has not been time. Daniel still initiates more often than she does; Megan and Avery alternate; Jess and Avery are roughly even.

She stopped drinking on January 4, 2026, and has not said why. The closest she has come is one sentence to her therapist in late January: "I want to be the one who knows what I am doing in the evenings." She has not used the word alcoholic and is not. She drank in the standard pattern of her professional class — two glasses of wine at dinner three or four nights a week, more on Fridays, occasional weekend cocktails — and stopped because she did not like what she was watching herself do in the second hour after Wren was down. Sam noticed the absence about two weeks in and asked once. She said she felt better. He has not asked again. She still pours sparkling water in a wine glass on Friday-night dinners and has begun to notice that she does it less performatively than she did in January.

On most days she would be hard to pity and easy to underestimate. Her competence has moved past the point where it is impressive and into the point where it is mostly invisible to the people working alongside her. She looks less tired than she is. The careful judgment of the people who know her well is that she is roughly as good as she thinks she is at the parts of her job she is good at, and noticeably less good than she thinks she is at the parts she has not yet had to confront. She is going to close this round. The harder material — the part of the company that will require her to be someone different than the person who got it to here — is on the other side of it, and the calendar has not yet given her room to look at it.
