# How Aisha Williams decides what matters this month

The single meeting that decides how the month goes is the strategic-pivot review with her SVP on May 28. It is forty-eight hours away. Everything else on her calendar sorts relative to that meeting — whether it makes it into prep, whether it gets handled in the seams, whether it gets ignored. The review is the one where the $180M slice of her surface either gets absorbed by the sister org under a different SVP in exchange for a new platform mandate, or it doesn't. Priya, her chief of staff who started in February, has built the deck three different ways. The narrative document is at the fourth revision. Rehearsals were on May 21 and are again on May 26. Aisha's position — that the trade is bad on the current terms and acceptable on the back-channel terms she has been working since February — has not been stated outside her own staff.

The second load-bearing item is the Q2 OKR retro narratives, which were due to the SVP's chief of staff on Friday May 22. Those went out clean. What did not go out clean is the consolidated area-level Q3 draft, which she owes the SVP for the June 3 portfolio review and which is currently six PM-lead inputs deep out of eight. Two leads are late. She has not chased them in writing yet. She will probably do it on Wednesday morning.

Sitting alongside the work items is Naima, twelve, finishing fifth grade at Palo Verde, and waiting on a JLS class-placement letter that was supposed to land the week of May 18 and has not. Aisha has been checking the JLS portal on her phone twice a day. Naima's closest friend Mira got a Mandarin Immersion placement at Greene Middle School and Naima found out about it by overhearing Mira's mother at school pickup. Aisha has been doing the Wednesday and Saturday bedtime check-ins. Idris had his third-grade play on May 21, which Aisha attended; field day was May 22, which Marcus took. The internal product-leadership summit panel on May 19 came and went. She did not prepare for it. She told Priya in the hallway that morning that she would wing it, and she did.

Same-hour responders are a small list. Her SVP gets a same-hour reply on anything, full stop — she has reported to him in different shapes since 2019 and he is the only person in the company who can change the agenda on May 28. Marcus gets a reply within ten minutes during the workday. Priya gets a reply within nine minutes if it's a business-hours Slack message, within ninety after dinner. Her eleven directs get the same nine-minute window. The two PM leads who are late with their Q3 input — Aisha has not chased them yet, which is itself a tell — would also be inside the nine-minute window if they wrote to her first. Peer VPs and the SVP's chief of staff get inside an hour. Janelle, her mother in southwest Atlanta, gets a text reply within an hour and a Sunday call at 4 p.m. Pacific without fail. Dara gets a reply when Aisha sees the message, which during exec-prep weeks is usually that evening.

The category of people she likes and gets to in two or three days is mostly the wider Google network — the peer VPs she does not have a council with this quarter, the former skip from her director years she still has dinner with twice a year, the two PM leaders who left in 2024 for Anthropic and Figma and run a long text thread with her. The text thread is currently nine days behind. Renée, the hospitalist at Stanford who is one of her two real friends, sent a photo of the families' July weekend plans on the 18th and Aisha replied on the 21st. None of this is unusual for the back half of May in a Google planning cycle and Renée knows it.

What she misses or notices late is more revealing. She subscribes to Stratechery and reads it daily; she subscribes to Lenny's Newsletter and reads it religiously. She subscribes to four other newsletters and has not opened them since February. Anything on LinkedIn that is not a recruiter writing about an open role on her team she sees on Saturday morning at the kitchen island if at all. She has not posted on LinkedIn since the last role she opened in February. She has not opened the JLS incoming-parents email list digest in two weeks even though she signed up for it in March. She does not read the Palo Alto Online schools coverage during exec-prep weeks; she will catch up on it in June. The HVAC compressor on the house is on borrowed time and Marcus has been getting bids since March; Aisha has not read the bids. She has told Marcus to pick one and she will sign off.

She has underweighted async messages relative to in-person ones consistently. Mei Tanaka, her last skip at the director level, told her in a final 1:1 in 2023 that "you write your way around the decisions you should be making." Aisha has quoted that sentence back to Marcus twice and to Dara once. The pattern has not changed since. Priya has been doing the same intervention work from a different angle — booking the rehearsal slots, forcing the deck to three versions instead of seven, refusing to take a fifth revision as a real instruction. The document on May 28 is at the fourth revision. There is not a fifth revision in the calendar. Priya is the reason for that.

The calendar does most of the structural work. Aisha is on campus Mondays, Wednesdays, and Thursdays, and at the kitchen island on Greer Road on Tuesdays and Fridays. She runs staff at 9 a.m. Monday with her eleven directs from the glass-walled office on the second floor of MP-9. The Tuesday 4 p.m. biweekly with the SVP is the meeting where the May 28 review actually gets steered; the formal review is the performance, and the 4 p.m. Tuesdays are the rehearsal of the politics. Wednesdays are skip-levels, two a week, with PM leads two layers down. Alternating Thursdays are the cross-org councils. Friday morning at the kitchen island is her best thinking block — the one written narrative that has to be hers, the hiring review for the director-level PM she will need to source if the May 28 outcome goes her way. Friday afternoon she takes a walk, listens to Hard Fork, and answers nothing.

The thirty minutes between dropping the kids at Palo Verde and the first meeting on her calendar is where the Slack inbox to the East Coast gets cleared. The hour between 9:15 and 10:30 at night, back at the kitchen island, is where the writing actually happens — the docs, the narrative, the comments on Priya's deck. Nothing strategic gets done at 4 p.m. Pacific. The 3-to-5 p.m. block on a Wednesday or Thursday is the block she is most likely to come out of having said something she will revise the next morning. Marcus has noticed she sends shorter Slack messages and uses fewer emoji during exec-prep weeks. Her SVP has noticed it twice and mentioned it once.

The current frictions are specific and worth naming because each is taking more attention than its actual stakes warrant. Sleep is the first. She has averaged six hours and ten minutes a night for the last three weeks per her Oura ring and was under five hours on two nights last week. The 10:30 bedtime is the one intervention she actually holds to and she has been holding to it; the wake-ups are unprompted and she does not know what to do with them. The kettlebell in the garage has been used twice in May. Next is the JLS placement letter, where the portal has not updated, Aisha has not emailed the school, and she has refreshed the portal on her phone before Monday staff at least twelve times in the last two weeks. Then there is Marcus, who has been quieter on Sunday evenings since the middle of April; he has not said anything about it and she has not asked. He raised the undivided-attention issue with her three times in 2026 — January, February, and a Friday in March — and each time she acknowledged it and adjusted for a week or two and slid back. He has not raised it in May. The last friction is mechanical: Priya has done the work of holding the deck to four revisions and the rehearsals to two, but Aisha has been adding small things to the read-ahead — a paragraph here, a footnote there — that Priya has had to triage out.

The other standing biases are easy to name and have been stable for years. Aisha overweights writing relative to speech; she trusts a clean narrative more than she trusts a clean meeting. She overweights people who have written something good for her at some point in the past; the two PM leads who followed her across the 2022 reorg are leads she sourced personally, and they get a longer leash on a missed week than the two leads who came in with the rebanding. She underweights people who deliver bad news verbally and well — a hallway conversation gets less action from her than a Slack message that says the same thing, even when she would acknowledge the hallway as the more honest channel. She gives the SVP more benefit of the doubt than she gives any peer VP, which is partly a function of seven years of working together and partly a function of the fact that he tells her the truth in writing.

The blind spot Dara has named, in 2022 over breakfast at Joanie's, is that Aisha presents her workload as the constraint everyone else is asked to schedule around and does not see how much of it she has generated herself. The extra rehearsal slot on the 26th was Aisha's idea, not Priya's. The fourth revision of the narrative document was Aisha's idea, not the SVP's. The pattern that will run the back half of June, once the May 28 review lands and Q3 planning resolves, is the same pattern as the back half of April. There are two open EM hires and a director-level PM to source if the platform mandate funds. The hiring will get done on Friday mornings at the kitchen island and not before.

Janelle's hip replacement is on September 14. The surgery week is already on the calendar at Piedmont Hospital. Priya has held the two days before and the two days after. Aisha will sleep in her childhood bedroom on Beecher Street that week and has not yet had the conversation with Jamal about whether she pays for a home health aide for the recovery. She will have it in July, probably from the rental house on the Mendocino coast, probably on the Friday morning of the week off. That is the kind of thing she handles in the seams of a vacation rather than in the seams of a workday, because the workday seams in May 2026 are already taken.
