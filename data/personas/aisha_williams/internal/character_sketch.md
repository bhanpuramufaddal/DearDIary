Aisha Marie Williams was born on March 11, 1984 at Grady Memorial Hospital in downtown Atlanta. She grew up at 1247 Beecher Street SW in the Cascade Heights neighborhood of southwest Atlanta, the older of two children. Her brother Jamal is three years younger and teaches eighth-grade math in DeKalb County. The Beecher Street house, a 1,950-square-foot brick ranch with a screened side porch and a back door that has stuck since the late nineties, is the house her mother Janelle still lives in. William and Janelle bought it in 1982 for $74,000 with a VA loan on William's Navy service. The mortgage was paid off in 2009.

Her father William Williams worked as a motor-vehicle inspector for the Georgia Department of Public Safety from 1979 until he retired in early 2014. He served in the Navy from 1968 to 1972, took an associate's degree at Atlanta Technical College on the GI Bill in his thirties, and never went any further with school. He was the parent who cooked weeknight dinners, paid the household bills out of a paper ledger he kept in the kitchen drawer, and drove Aisha and Jamal to school every morning until they could walk. He was quiet, not angry. Janelle taught high school English at D.M. Therrell from 1985 until 1998, moved to Benjamin E. Mays High School as an assistant principal that year, and was the principal at Mays from 2008 until her retirement in 2022. <!-- web-verified 2026-05-26: Benjamin E. She was also the household's primary earner from about the time Aisha started middle school onward, though no one in the family ever framed it that way.

Money in the Beecher Street house was steady but watched. William and Janelle did not refinance the house when rates dropped in the late nineties because William did not trust the paperwork. They drove the same Buick LeSabre from 1997 until 2008. Aisha got her first paying job — folding T-shirts at the Gap at Greenbriar Mall — at fifteen, and was expected to put half her checks into a savings account at Citizens Trust that Janelle had set up for her in 1992. Aisha still has the account. The balance sat at $2,341 the last time she logged in, in March 2024.

She attended Mays for high school from 1998 to 2002, which meant being a student at the school where her mother was an assistant principal for all four years. She ran cross-country, played second-chair clarinet in the wind ensemble, was treasurer of the Beta Club, and graduated fourth in a class of 412. She was not popular and was not unpopular. The first time she was good at something in a way other people noticed was the spring of tenth grade, when she placed second in a regional Future Business Leaders of America case competition in Macon and her mother drove home with her in the same car without saying a word about it. Janelle had told her on the way down to Macon that the school had paid her registration fee out of a fund Janelle herself oversaw, and that she expected Aisha to do well. Aisha has told this story to Marcus exactly once, in the kitchen on Greer in 2017, and has not told it to anyone else.

She matriculated at Spelman College in fall 2002, took a BA in economics in 2006, and graduated cum laude. The decision to go to Spelman rather than to a school out of state was largely her mother's; Janelle had taken Aisha to Spelman's open house in October of her senior year of high school and the application went in three weeks later. <!-- web-verified 2026-05-26: Spelman College, historically Black women's liberal arts college in Atlanta, GA --> Aisha lived in Manley her freshman year, in an off-campus apartment on Westview Drive after that, and worked twenty hours a week at the campus career center her junior and senior years. She interned at SunTrust in Atlanta the summer after sophomore year and at McKinsey's Atlanta office the summer after junior year. McKinsey extended her a full-time offer in December 2005.

She worked at McKinsey in Atlanta from August 2006 to July 2008 as a business analyst, doing mostly consumer-goods and retail-banking engagements out of the Peachtree office. She matriculated at NYU Stern's full-time MBA program in fall 2008 and graduated in May 2010. <!-- web-verified 2026-05-26: NYU Stern School of Business, Manhattan, offers a full-time MBA program --> She lived in a two-bedroom on East 11th Street with Dara Okafor for both years of the program. She met Marcus Williams at a Black engineers' mixer at a coworking space in DUMBO on a Saturday afternoon in November 2008 — he was at Carnegie Mellon for his master's in robotics and had come down to New York for a Google recruiting trip. They were exclusive by February 2009 and engaged in October 2010. They married on June 18, 2011 at the AME church on Cascade Road in Atlanta, with sixty guests; Janelle planned it.

Aisha rejoined McKinsey, in New York, after the MBA. She left in October 2011 to take a senior PM role at Square — the recruiter was a Stern classmate two years ahead of her — and she and Marcus moved to San Francisco in January 2012. They rented in Hayes Valley for the first year and a half, moved to a house in Glen Park in 2013 when she joined Google, and bought the Greer Road house in late 2017 when Aisha was a year into being a director and Naima was four. Naima was born at California Pacific Medical Center on April 9, 2014. Idris was born at Stanford Hospital on August 22, 2016.

Her father died in the fall of 2016. William was diagnosed with stage-four pancreatic adenocarcinoma at Emory University Hospital in February of that year and died at the hospice on Clairmont Road on October 4, 2016, six weeks before Idris was born. Aisha flew back to Atlanta seven times over the course of those eight months, mostly on Friday-night red-eyes. She was on bedrest for the last six weeks of the pregnancy and could not fly down for the funeral; Marcus went with Naima. The week she went back into the office at Google after maternity leave, in January 2017, she asked her then-director if she could be staffed off the launch project she had been running, and she has never said publicly what the reason was. She was on 50 mg of sertraline by the summer of 2019 and has been on it since. She has told her internist at PAMF that the sertraline started after Idris was born; she has not described to the internist what else happened that year.

She has not been back to the hospice on Clairmont Road and has not asked her mother about William's last weeks in any detail. Jamal has the keys to the storage unit in East Point with William's tools and the unopened boxes from his desk at the DPS. Aisha has not been to the storage unit. Janelle has, twice.

The career arc since then has been steady and externally legible. She made director at Google in March 2018, senior director in April 2021, and VP in October 2024. The promotion to VP came at the end of a stretch where she had run the same product surface through two reorgs and a model-platform shift, and where she had built a credible internal reputation for being the person you wanted writing the doc when the doc had to land. She is, in fact, very good at writing docs. Her SVP — the same person she has reported to in different shapes since 2019 — has said in two consecutive year-end reviews that her written narrative is the clearest in his org. She is also genuinely good at hiring; six of her current eight PM leads were people she sourced personally, and three of them followed her across the 2022 reorg.

She is less good than people assume at execution under ambiguity. She knows this about herself in the sense that she can describe it when asked directly, though she would not volunteer it. Her tendency under pressure is to retreat into structure — more meetings, longer docs, more carefully drawn boxes on slides — when the problem actually calls for someone in the room making a fast call with bad information. Her last skip at the director level, a woman named Mei Tanaka who left for a CPO role at a Series C in 2023, told her in a final 1:1 that "you write your way around the decisions you should be making." Aisha has quoted that sentence back to Marcus twice and to Dara once. She has not changed the behavior, though she catches herself doing it more often than she used to. Priya, her new chief of staff, has been doing some of the same intervention work that Mei used to do, more gently and from a different angle.

The work surface itself, week to week, is what people inside Google call inside-the-machine. Mondays at nine she runs staff with her eleven directs from her glass-walled office on the second floor of MP-9. Tuesdays and Fridays she works from the kitchen island on Greer Road, in a rotation of cardigans over the same five black T-shirts, with the dog Tilda asleep under the stool. She does her two skip-level 1:1s on Wednesdays and her biweekly with the SVP at 4 p.m. Tuesdays. She is on campus three days a week and is in twenty-three meetings on a typical Monday. She answers her direct reports' Slack messages within nine minutes during business hours and within ninety minutes after dinner. Peer VPs and the SVP get same-hour response time. Her own boss has noticed that when Aisha is in exec-prep mode she sends shorter Slack messages and uses fewer emoji.

She and Marcus are well-matched, and they are also tired. They have been married fifteen years next month. They run a household well — the shared Notes document, the Sunday-afternoon calendar review, the seven-block walk to Palo Verde — and they have the kind of marriage in which the logistics are never the problem. Marcus's organizing principle is logistical reliability and he delivers it; he is the one who knows when the smog check is due on the Volvo and when the AYSO uniform money has to be in. Aisha's organizing principle is undivided attention and she does not always deliver it. Marcus has raised this with her in January, in February, and again on a Friday in March of 2026 — three times so far this year. Each time she has acknowledged it and adjusted for a week or two and slid back. They are not in a crisis. They are also not in a marriage that runs on the same fuel it ran on in 2015. Marcus has been quieter on Sunday evenings than the rest of the week through April and into May. He has not said anything about it. She has not asked.

She is not religious. She grew up in the AME church on Cascade Road and went every Sunday until she left for Spelman; she has been back only for funerals since. She has two blind spots that the people closest to her would name quickly. The first is that she presents her workload as the constraint that everyone in her life is asked to schedule around, and she does not see how much of that workload is self-generated — extra rehearsal slots, fourth and fifth doc revisions, calls she could let her directs run. The mundane texture of who she is. She eats Greek yogurt with frozen blueberries and a tablespoon of peanut butter for breakfast on Mondays through Thursdays, and the same egg-and-cheese on a toasted English muffin she has eaten on Fridays since 2018. She listens to the Hard Fork podcast on Friday afternoon walks and Acquired on long drives; in the car alone she puts on Lauryn Hill, Sade, or, increasingly, the early Beyoncé country tracks. She answers texts from her mother within an hour, from Marcus within ten minutes during the workday, from Dara when she sees them. She is a fast and decisive grocery shopper and a terrible chooser of restaurants. She picks at her cuticles when she's on a call she finds tedious. She drinks two coffees a day, both before noon, and does not drink in the evenings during exec-prep weeks. On Sunday afternoons when nothing is scheduled she sits in the armchair by the front window with whatever novel she is reading — currently the Min Jin Lee — and falls asleep within about twenty minutes. She is a person other people remember after a wedding because she asks them a real question early and then actually listens to the answer. She is not the person who stays at the wedding past eleven; she and Marcus are out by ten-fifteen, every time, and the babysitter is paid by ten-thirty.

She is forty-one. She is a VP of Product at Google, a job neither of her parents ever heard her describe before she had it. She has not started any specific planning for what she will do when the kids are out of the house. She has not been back to Atlanta since Christmas. She will be there in September.
